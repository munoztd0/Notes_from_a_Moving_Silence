# Sea Rain. 

Mimitsu-machi has been left untouched since 1830, and it's the place where the first emperor of Japan, Jimmu, 2600 years ago, set sail from there to unite Japan. I just came there because it was close to the most famous surf breaks of Jpapan. But I was fortunate enough to meet this guy, Keizo.

A middle-aged surfer, retired, he's guided most surfers from all around the world. His English was a little rusted, and it was sometimes difficult to even understand his Google translations, but he was the greatest Japanese person I've met so far. He was burned by the sun, which is very uncommon to see in Japan, as they are all very cautious about the sun.

Plus, having been on many foreigners for several years, you can see that his Japanese manners had certainly been corroded by Western surfer life. He explained to me how he got knocked out from an 6-meter storm wave, which dislocated both of his knees three years ago, and that he hasn't been able to surf since then. But that this weekend, he was going to surf again for the first time in three years with me. 

I felt very honored, but I was also scared that he would bring me to a 14 feet wave break at the same time. Anyhow, it was my first day, and my face was battered by the shores of the Miyazaki prefecture. Suffice to say, the sea wasn't calm.
There I was, waiting for the waves to jump on me and my board. I was just amazed by the sheer size of the break.

You could count over 50 surfers riding along, and that was a big thing. The sun had just set on the sea about half an hour ago, but the clouds were rolling in. They were robbing us of a clear sense of direction. They were flashing like a megawatt each minute or so. Instead, we were soon welcoming the morning sea rain. What a feeling.. Sensing the droplets leaping onto your face, while you're paddling out in the sea rain.

Thanks Keizo !

# Best meal of my life.

It was my first and last night, staying at Mimitsu-machi, the old samurai town of Edo.

The temperature was way too low to surf, I decided to invite Keizo for a last meal at the neighborhood izakaya. The name was Hinataya.

From outside, it did look like old buildings in the old town, a 19th-century traditional samurai house. But the inside was totally different.

It was pretty hipster-y, with an old-school wooden pendant clock, a cute illustration, and 70s rock ballads playing. I wasn't shocked. The cutest thing were their menus depicting cute little twins and saying something like, "Don't mind the two little monsters if you see them running around."

I quickly understood that it was not your regular sports izakaya with drunken dudes sipping on shochu. No, it was a refined and classy contemporary restaurant run by a young couple that fled from Tokyo's noise to live there by themselves in a small hotel basement, raising the two little terrors by the sound of the waves crashing on the shore. I asked Keizo about his menu and recommendations, and he smiled at me and said, "Everything is good" with a big smile.

So I started decrypting the Japanese menu, Keizo went ahead and ordered a platter of sashimi and tempura fried fish, to start off. I ordered my usual drink here in the Kyushu peninsula, and a yuzu cider, a sort of Japanese soda with a hint of yuzu.

Amazing. My fish platter was already almost finished, and I wasn't really excited on what to order, but something caught my eye. A whole "pata de jamón ibérico" was there laying behind the bar.

I asked the owner, "Is this from Japan?" And he said, "No, no, no, this is from Spain." I started telling him that I was from Spain, but they were quite confused, since Keizo told them that I was from Switzerland like 10 minutes ago. Anyway, for a second I thought I was ordering a platter of "Berota". That's how Japanese call "Bellota", because they don't have the L sound, which I found specially hilarious. But when I saw the price, I quickly backed off. And yeah, I didn't come all the way here to eat jamón.

What did I order then, you ask me? Well, first I ordered scallops with asparagus, but of course they were all out of it. That was maybe one of the best misfortunes I ever had, because after that I ordered the yam teppanyaki, on the side of their special charcoal-grilled chicken fried rice. Nothing too fancy.

By that time I already had a bajillion fried rice dishes during my Japanese adventures, and a billion teppanyaki over my time in the Kansai area. But oh god, they here it was different. I finally understood the term "soul food".
Fried rice is maybe one of the most popular foods all over Eastern Asia, and also one of the simplest dishes ever. But man, the charcoal-grilled chicken had so much flavor from the smokiness and the umaminess. Everything was just so balanced.

And the rice, it didn't look like anything. It was cooked to perfection. Not too dry, not too oily, not too chunky, not too boiled. The veggies were barely cooked, just the perfect crunchiness. There were some fresh radishes cut in very small cubes, and that brought some very much appreciated freshness.
Per-fe-ction. It felt like I was a ten-year-old boy coming out of school in rural Japan and getting served a bowl of love by my Japanese grandma. But wait, that was not all.

I was served with this yam teppanyaki in its iron pan. It was smoking hot. It looked like a Spanish tortilla made love to an American pancake, but inside it was a pool of cheese, which was not very common to see in Japan. And it was so fluffy, like if someone gently touched the inside of your mouth with a sponge. It was very simple, but the Japanese yam, which is not very sweet like we are used to in Western countries, and the daikon, Japanese white radishes, cooked to perfection, and the flavors, again, balanced with the utmost delicacy and respect, just melted my heart. I kept on making this "mmmhh" sound you usually make to be polite, or when you don't know the language, and you want to express appreciation, but all of them, every single one, were true, and couldn't be helped.
After savoring each bite of my meal, I smiled and felt warmness and tenderness inside of me. I couldn't express what I was feeling in Japanese. It was way more complicated than my basic Japanese.
It's even still hard to describe it in English right now..
