> Best meal of my life. 
I was definitely not expecting this. It was my first and last night, staying at Mimitsu-machi, the old samurai town of Edo.
> This place has been left untouched since 1830, and it's the place where the first emperor of Japan, Jimmu, 2600 years ago, set sail from there to unite Japan. He just came there because it was close to the most famous breaks. But I was fortunate enough to meet this guy, Keizo.
> A middle-aged surfer, retiring, he's guided most surfers from all around the world. His English was a little interesting, and it was sometimes difficult to even understand his Google translations, but he was the greatest Japanese person I've met so far. He was burned by the sun, which is very uncommon to see in Japan, as they are all very cautious about the sun, but he here, as a surfer, definitely showed it.
> Plus, having been on many boards for several years, you can see that his Japanese manners had certainly been corroded by Western surfer life. He explained to me how he got knocked out from an 8-meter storm wave, which dislocated both of his shoulders three years ago, and that he hasn't been able to surf since then. But at this weekend, he was going to surf again for the first time in three years with me.
> I felt very honored, but I was also scared that he would bring me to a 5-meter storm wave break at the same time. Anyhow, it was my last day, and my phone was battered by the shores of the Miyazaki prefecture. Suffice to say, the sea wasn't calm.
> The temperature was way too low. On the day to surf, I decided to invite Keizo for a last meal at the neighborhood izakaya. The name was Hinataya.
> Hinataya is French. From outside, it did look like old buildings in the old town, a 19th-century traditional samurai house. But the inside was totally different.
> It was pretty hipster-y, with an old-school wooden pendant clock, a cute illustration, and 70s rock ballads playing. I wasn't shocked. The cutest thing were their menus depicting cute little twins and saying something like, "Don't mind the two little monsters if you see them running around."
> I quickly understood that it was not your regular sports izakaya with drunken dudes sipping on shochu. No, it was a refined and classy contemporary restaurant run by a young couple that fled from Tokyo's noise to live there on their own in a small hotel basement, raising the two little terrors by the sound of the waves crashing on the shore. I asked Keizo about his menu and recommendations, and he smiled at me and said, "Everything is good."
> So I started decrypting the Japanese menu, but it was so annoying that I went ahead and ordered a platter of sashimi and tempura. Tempura fried fish, to start off. I ordered my usual drink, a draft beer, and a yuzu cider, a sort of Japanese soda with a hint of yuzu.
> Amazing. My fish platter was already almost finished, and I wasn't really excited on what to order, but something caught my eye. A whole pata de jamón ibérico was there laying behind the bar.
> I asked the owner, "Is this from Japan?" And he said, "No, no, no, this is from Spain." I started telling them that I was from Spain, but they were quite confused, since I told them that I went there like 10 years ago. Anyway, for a second I thought I was ordering a platter of hamon.
> That's how Japanese call it, because they don't have the J sound, which I found hilarious. But when I saw the price, I quickly backed off. And yeah, I didn't come all the way here to eat jamón.
> What did I fucking order then, you ask me? Well, first I ordered scallops with asparagus, but of course they were all out of it. That was maybe one of the best misfortunes I ever had, because after that I ordered the yam teppanyaki, on the side of the famous charcoal-grilled chicken fried rice. Nothing too fancy.
> I had a bajillion fried rice dishes during my Japanese adventures, and a billion teppanyaki over my steps in Osaka and Hiroshima. But oh god, they were different. I finally needed to turn to soul food.
> Fried rice is maybe one of the most popular foods all over Eastern Asia, and also one of the simplest dishes ever. But man, the charcoal-grilled chicken had so much flavor from the smokiness and the umaminess. Everything was just so balanced.
> And the rice, it didn't look like anything. It was cooked to perfection. Not too dry, not too oily, not too chalky, not too burnt. The veggies were barely cooked, just the perfect crunchiness. There were some fresh radishes cut in very small... in very small... cubes, and that brought some freshness.
> Perfection. It felt like I was a ten-year-old boy coming out of school in rural Japan and getting served a bowl of love by my Japanese grandma. But wait, that was not all.
> I was served this yam teppanyaki in its iron pan. It was smoking hot. It looked like a Spanish tortilla had sex with an American pancake, but inside it was a pool of cheese, which was not very common to see in Japan.
> And it was so fluffy, like if someone gently touched the inside of your mouth with a sponge. It was very simple, but the element of Japanese yam, which is not very sweet like we are used to in Western countries, and daikon, Japanese white radishes, cooked to perfection, and the flavors, again, balanced with the utmost delicacy and respect, just melted my heart. I kept on making this mmm sound you usually make to be polite, or when you don't know the language, and you want to express appreciation, but all of them, every single one, was true, and couldn't be helped.
> After savoring each bite of my meal, I smiled and felt warmness and tenderness inside of me. I couldn't express what I was feeling in Japanese. It was way more complicated than my basic Japanese.
> It's even hard to describe it in English right now.
