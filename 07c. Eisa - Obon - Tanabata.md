The Oonahiki in Tokashiki traditionally pits the East (Agari) against the West (Iri). In many Okinawan coastal villages, this also symbolizes a friendly rivalry between the Farmers and the Fishermen, with the outcome sometimes being used to "predict" whether the next year will bring better harvests or better catches.
The preparation and the rope itself involve specific parts and rituals:
The Rope (Ufunaa)
The massive rope is not a single piece but two distinct sections joined together just before the pull.
 * Meenau (Female Rope): The rope representing the West. It has a large loop at the end.
 * Oonau (Male Rope): The rope representing the East. It features a thick, straight "head" that is inserted into the Meenau’s loop.
 * Kanuchi-bou: A thick wooden pole (the linchpin) that is dropped through the joined ropes to lock them together. This "union" is the climax of the pre-game ceremony.
Construction & Materials
Preparation usually begins weeks in advance, involving the entire community:
 * Rice Straw (Wara): Thousands of bundles of rice straw are twisted together. The core is often reinforced with older rope or thicker strands to handle the immense tension.
 * Teemichi: These are smaller "hand-ropes" attached to the main thick trunk. Since the main rope is too thick to grip, participants pull on these side branches.
 * Kachiruu: The act of braiding and tightening the rope. In Tokashiki, this is a communal labor where elders pass down the specific twisting techniques to younger generations.
Symbolic Elements
 * Hatagashira: Large, ornamental banners representing each side. They are carried by strong young men and danced up and down to rally the pullers.
 * Shitaku: Two individuals dressed as legendary figures or warriors who stand atop the joined ropes at the center, facing each other just before the signal to pull is given.


The Tug-of-War festival held on Tokashiki Island in the Kerama Islands is called Oonahiki (or Oonahiki Matsuri).
While many areas in Okinawa hold tug-of-war events, the one in Tokashiki is a significant cultural tradition usually held during the lunar August full moon period (typically September or October) to pray for a bountiful harvest and the health of the villagers.


The Eisa dance on Tokashiki has a fascinating backstory. It’s not just a performance; it’s a "revived" tradition with a bit of a legendary trade-off behind it.
While the Tug-of-War (Oonahiki) is an ancient staple, the specific Eisa dance you see today was actually lost for a long time—likely due to the devastation of WWII. 

The Performance (Michi Junee)
During the Obon festival (lunar July), the troupe performs a "Michi Junee" (street procession). Their role is spiritual: they use the drums to guide ancestors' spirits to the correct houses.
 * Day 1 (Unke): They perform in the villages of Tokashiki and Tokashiku to welcome the spirits.
 * Day 2 (Nakabi): They move to Aharen to continue the celebration.
The Crew
The lineup consists of three main roles:
 * Jikata: The musicians who play the Sanshin (the three-stringed Okinawan lute) and lead the singing.
 * Uchite (Drummers): * **Odaiko: Large barrel drums slung over the shoulder. These drummers provide the "heartbeat" and perform powerful, sweeping movements.
   * Shimedaiko: Smaller, hand-held drums. These dancers are usually more agile and perform faster, more intricate footwork.
 * Chondara: These are the "clowns" of the group. They wear white face paint and black-and-white striped outfits. While they look funny, they are actually the most experienced members—they act as conductors, keeping the lines straight, handing out water, and energizing the crowd.
Unique Flavor
They often stop to perform in front of newly opened businesses for luck or at houses where a family member recently passed away to honor their spirit.

1. The Timeline and Links: Okinawan Summer Festivals
In places like Tokashiki, these events are deeply interconnected and follow the lunar calendar, forming a complete cycle of welcoming the past (ancestors) and securing the future (harvest/health).
 * Step 1: Tanabata (7th day of the 7th lunar month)
   * The Role: The Preparation.
   * The Link: It acts as the prelude to Obon. Families clean the graves and literally clear the physical and spiritual path for the ancestors to return home.
 * Step 2: Obon (13th–15th days of the 7th lunar month)
   * The Role: The Reunion.
   * The Link: The ancestors arrive. For three days, families feast, spend time together, and honor the spirits of the departed who are visiting the earthly realm.
 * Step 3: Eisa (During Obon)
   * The Role: The Guide and Celebration.
   * The Link: Eisa is the soundtrack and the engine of Obon. The drummers and dancers parade through the streets (Michi Junee) to welcome the spirits, entertain them during their stay, and ultimately drum them safely back to the afterlife on the final night.
 * Step 4: Oonahiki / Tug-of-War (Usually the 8th lunar month)
   * The Role: The Future.
   * The Link: Once the ancestors are safely sent back, the community pivots from honoring the dead to securing luck for the living. The East vs. West (Farmers vs. Fishermen) tug-of-war is held to predict and pray for a bountiful harvest and good health for the upcoming year.
2. The Fable of Tanabata (The Star Festival)
The legend of Tanabata originates from a Chinese myth and centers on two stars: Vega and Altair.
 * The Lovers: Orihime (the Weaving Princess) was a diligent weaver who made beautiful clothes for her father, the Emperor of Heaven. Hikoboshi (the Cowherd) was a hardworking farmer who tended to the herds of heaven.
 * The Conflict: The Emperor introduced them, and they fell deeply in love. However, they were so enamored with each other that they completely abandoned their duties. Orihime's loom gathered dust, and Hikoboshi's cows wandered wildly across the heavens.
 * The Punishment: Furious at their negligence, the Emperor separated the lovers, placing them on opposite sides of the Amanogawa (the Milky Way river), forbidding them to meet.
 * The Compromise: Orihime wept so much that her father relented. He decreed that if they both worked hard and fulfilled their duties throughout the year, they would be allowed to meet for just one night: the 7th day of the 7th month.
 * The Magpie Bridge: Legend says that on this night, if the skies are clear, a flock of magpies flies up to form a bridge over the Milky Way so the lovers can reunite. If it rains, the river floods, the birds cannot form the bridge, and the lovers must wait another year.
