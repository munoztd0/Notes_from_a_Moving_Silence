The Oonahiki in Tokashiki traditionally pits the East (Agari) against the West (Iri). In many Okinawan coastal villages, this also symbolizes a friendly rivalry between the Farmers and the Fishermen, with the outcome sometimes being used to "predict" whether the next year will bring better harvests or better catches.
The preparation and the rope itself involve specific parts and rituals:
The Rope (Ufunaa)
The massive rope is not a single piece but two distinct sections joined together just before the pull.
 * Meenau (Female Rope): The rope representing the West. It has a large loop at the end.
 * Oonau (Male Rope): The rope representing the East. It features a thick, straight "head" that is inserted into the Meenau’s loop.
 * Kanuchi-bou: A thick wooden pole (the linchpin) that is dropped through the joined ropes to lock them together. This "union" is the climax of the pre-game ceremony.
Construction & Materials
Preparation usually begins weeks in advance, involving the entire community:
 * Rice Straw (Wara): Thousands of bundles of rice straw are twisted together. The core is often reinforced with older rope or thicker strands to handle the immense tension.
 * Teemichi: These are smaller "hand-ropes" attached to the main thick trunk. Since the main rope is too thick to grip, participants pull on these side branches.
 * Kachiruu: The act of braiding and tightening the rope. In Tokashiki, this is a communal labor where elders pass down the specific twisting techniques to younger generations.
Symbolic Elements
 * Hatagashira: Large, ornamental banners representing each side. They are carried by strong young men and danced up and down to rally the pullers.
 * Shitaku: Two individuals dressed as legendary figures or warriors who stand atop the joined ropes at the center, facing each other just before the signal to pull is given.


The Tug-of-War festival held on Tokashiki Island in the Kerama Islands is called Oonahiki (or Oonahiki Matsuri).
While many areas in Okinawa hold tug-of-war events, the one in Tokashiki is a significant cultural tradition usually held during the lunar August full moon period (typically September or October) to pray for a bountiful harvest and the health of the villagers.
